    title="A Region Under Water: The Greater Horn's Flood Crisis"
    dateStart="2019-11"
    dateEnd="2024-10"
    center="37.2, 3.0"
    zoom="4.5"
    pitch="25"
    bearing="8"
  >
    Between 2019 and 2024, the Greater Horn of Africa endured a relentless cycle of
    catastrophic flooding that affected over **5.3 million people**, displaced nearly
    **1.9 million**, and claimed at least **450 lives** across 11 countries.

    The floods were not random. They were driven by compounding climate forces — an
    exceptionally strong El Niño, a positive Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD), and intensifying
    seasonal rainfall patterns — converging on landscapes scarred by deforestation, rapid
    urbanisation, and decades of under-investment in drainage and flood defences.

    From the highland rivers of Ethiopia to the flat floodplains of South Sudan, from the
    arid wadis of Djibouti to the shores of Lake Victoria, the pattern was the same:
    unprecedented rainfall overwhelming communities that had little time and fewer resources
    to prepare. This storyline traces that arc, country by country.

    dateStart="2023-10"

    When the October–December 2023 short rains arrived supercharged by El Niño, Burundi's
    densely settled lakeside and riverine communities bore the brunt. Torrential downpours
    swelled Lake Tanganyika and its tributaries, inundating low-lying neighbourhoods in
    Bujumbura and rural communes across the western provinces.

    More than **203,000 people** were directly affected. Over **98,000** were forced from
    their homes, many into makeshift camps with no sanitation. An estimated **19,250 houses**
    were destroyed — not merely damaged, but reduced to rubble by floodwaters and mudslides.
    The humanitarian response triggered an anticipatory action activation to pre-position
    relief supplies before the worst of the season arrived.

  <Chapter
    title="Djibouti: A City Submerged in Hours"
    dateStart="2019-11-21"
    dateEnd="2019-11-23"
    admin0="DJI"
    center="42.5903, 11.8251"
    zoom="7.4"
    pitch="15"
  >
    On 21 November 2019, roughly **300 mm of rain** fell on Djibouti City in a matter of
    hours — nearly three times the country's entire annual average. The city's rudimentary
    drainage network was overwhelmed almost instantly. Streets became rivers. Homes built on
    low ground were submerged to their rooftops.

    The disaster killed **9 people**, including children swept away by flash currents, and
    affected an estimated **250,000** — more than a quarter of the country's population. For
    a small, arid nation accustomed to drought rather than deluge, the event was a stark
    reminder that climate extremes cut both ways. Satellite imagery captured the full extent
    of inundation across the capital, revealing water standing in areas that had been dry
    for decades.

    During April–May 2024, the wider eastern Africa rainfall system brought heavy and
    sustained precipitation to Eritrea, placing vulnerable communities — particularly in
    the western lowlands and along seasonal watercourses — at heightened flood risk. While
    ground-level reporting from Eritrea remains limited, regional monitoring by OCHA flagged
    the country alongside its neighbours as part of the broader heavy-rain emergency
    affecting the Horn.

    The episode underscored the interconnected nature of the region's flood risk: the same
    atmospheric moisture that devastated Kenya and Ethiopia during this period pushed
    dangerous rainfall totals into Eritrea's normally drier zones.

  <Chapter
    title="Ethiopia: Highland Torrents Cascade Across Seven Regions"
    dateStart="2024-04"
    dateEnd="2024-05"
    admin0="ETH"
    central_point="40.4897, 9.145"
    zoom="5.8"

  >
    Ethiopia's 2024 rainy season delivered some of the most widespread flood impacts the
    country had seen in years. Heavy highland rainfall cascaded down through river systems
    into lowland communities across **seven regions** — Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Sidama,
    Somali, South Ethiopia, and Tigray — each already grappling with displacement from
    conflict or drought.

    More than **590,000 people** were affected and around **95,000 displaced**, many for
    the second or third time. Agricultural land was inundated at a critical point in the
    growing season, threatening food security in areas that could least afford further
    shocks. The scale of simultaneous flooding across such a vast and topographically
    diverse country highlighted how deeply embedded flood vulnerability has become in
    Ethiopia's development landscape.

  >
    Kenya's March–May 2024 long rains were catastrophic. Fuelled by residual El Niño
    warmth in the Indian Ocean, rainfall totals far exceeded seasonal norms, sending the
    Tana, Nzoia, and other major rivers into record-breaking flood. Nairobi's Mathare
    and Mukuru slums were inundated; highways linking the capital to the coast were severed
    by landslides and bridge collapses.

    The human toll was devastating: **188 deaths**, **165,000 people displaced**, and
    **90 people reported missing** — many swept away by flash floods or buried under
    collapsed structures. The disaster disrupted transport, commerce, and schooling for
    weeks and prompted a national emergency response. It also reignited debate about
    unregulated construction in flood-prone riparian zones.

  <Chapter
    title="Rwanda: Floods and Landslides Claim Over a Hundred Lives"
    dateStart="2023-05-01"
    dateEnd="2023-05-03"
    admin0="RWA"
    center="29.8739, -1.9403"
    zoom="7.5"
    pitch="30"
    bearing="-10"
  >
    In early May 2023, intense rainfall over Rwanda's steep, densely cultivated hillsides
    triggered a deadly combination of flash floods and landslides. Over the course of just
    two days, entire hillside communities were buried or swept away.

    The death toll reached between **109 and 131 people** — one of the highest from a
    single flood event in the region. A further **51,905 people** were directly affected
    and **5,472 houses destroyed**. Rwanda's hilly terrain, high population density, and
    intensive land use make it uniquely vulnerable to compound flood-landslide disasters,
    where saturated soils on steep slopes give way without warning. The IFRC launched a
    DREF emergency operation to support survivors.

    Somalia suffered the most severe flooding in the region. The October–December 2023
    Deyr season — amplified by one of the strongest El Niño events on record — sent the
    Shabelle and Juba rivers to levels not seen in decades. The Shabelle breached its banks
    along hundreds of kilometres, inundating farmland, settlements, and displacement camps
    already hosting families uprooted by drought and conflict.

    The numbers are staggering: **2.48 million people** affected, over **1.2 million
    displaced**, and estimated damages of approximately **USD 193.4 million**. Before
    communities could recover, the 2024 Gu rains brought a second wave of flash flooding.
    The compounding nature of these shocks — drought followed by flood, displacement upon
    displacement — has left Somalia's coping capacity critically overstretched.

  <Chapter
    title="South Sudan: The Nile's Slow-Moving Catastrophe"
    dateStart="2024-09"
    dateEnd="2024-10"
    admin0="SSD"
    centralPoint="30.2176, 6.877"
    zoom="6.1"
    pitch="10"
    mapOverlays='[{"url":"/storylines/geojson/2020-juba-white-nile-floods.geojson","label":"White Nile floodplain","color":"#0ea5e9","layer_type":"fill","opacity":0.38}]'
  >
    South Sudan's floods are not sudden — they creep. Driven by seasonal rainfall across
    the vast Nile basin, elevated upstream lake levels, and the slow rise of the White Nile
    and its tributaries, floodwaters spread across the country's low-lying plains over weeks
    and months, turning an area roughly the size of **48,000 km²** — larger than Denmark —
    into standing water.

    By October 2024, **1.4 million people** were affected and **379,000 displaced**, many
    into areas that themselves were at risk of inundation. The floods destroyed crops,
    contaminated water sources, and cut off entire communities from roads and markets.
    For a country already enduring conflict, food insecurity, and one of the world's
    largest internal displacement crises, the annual flood season has become an additional
    layer of compounding hardship.

    Sudan entered its 2024 rainy season already in the grip of a devastating civil war.
    When heavy rains arrived in July–August, they struck a population with virtually no
    institutional capacity to respond. Flooding hit Kassala, North Darfur, and Sennar
    states hardest, destroying over **1,000 houses**, killing at least **5 people**, and
    displacing thousands more into an already overwhelmed displacement system.

    The floods exposed a grim reality: in a country where conflict has destroyed
    infrastructure, scattered government services, and driven millions from their homes,
    even a moderate flood season becomes a humanitarian emergency. Aid access was
    constrained by active fighting, making response slow and incomplete.

    From November 2023 through April 2024, Tanzania experienced a prolonged flood season
    driven by the combined influence of El Niño and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole. The
    Lake Victoria basin, coastal lowlands, and river valleys bore repeated episodes of
    heavy rainfall, each compounding the damage of the last.

    The toll was severe: **155 deaths**, **236 injuries**, and around **200,000 people
    affected** across roughly 51,000 households. Landslides in the highlands added to the
    destruction, burying homes and severing roads. The extended duration of the event —
    stretching across two rainy seasons — meant that communities had no respite between
    flood episodes, leaving damaged infrastructure unrepaired and displaced families
    unable to return.

    Throughout 2023, Uganda experienced a relentless succession of floods and landslides
    as El Niño-driven storms swept across the country's diverse terrain — from the steep
    slopes of the Rwenzori foothills to the flat shores of Lake Victoria. Ten districts
    were particularly hard hit, each marked on the map.

    Between January and September 2023, **97,727 people** were affected and over **7,900
    displaced**. In mountainous western districts like Kabale and Kanungu, saturated
    hillsides gave way in devastating landslides. In the eastern districts of Bukedea and
    Namisindwa, river flooding inundated farmland at the height of the planting season.
    The geographic spread of the impacts — from the northwest (Zombo) to the Lake Victoria
    islands (Kalangala) — illustrates how no part of the country was spared.
